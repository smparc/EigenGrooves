\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Music is something everyone can feel. Pinning that feeling to a universal
standard has proved difficult, so we classify music by genre instead. This
project asks a different question: if we classify by measurable audio features
--- valence, energy, tempo --- does the resulting organisation reproduce the
genre taxonomy, or replace it?

We build a music recommendation system on a singular value decomposition
implemented from first principles, projecting songs into a reduced latent space
and ranking by cosine similarity within it. Two design decisions receive
particular attention. We use one-sided Jacobi rather than the textbook
eigendecomposition of $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$, which squares the condition number and
destroys the trailing singular values of a correlated feature matrix: on
representative data the two differ by eight orders of magnitude in relative
accuracy ($\jacobiMaxRelErr$ against $\eighMaxRelErr$). And we select the
number of latent dimensions by explicit criteria rather than by convention.

We then evaluate the system, which is the part most such projects omit. Against
random, popularity and no-decomposition baselines, using paired bootstrap
tests, we find that dimensionality reduction does not improve retrieval
accuracy. At $k=7$ the difference from plain cosine similarity on the scaled
features is not statistically distinguishable ($\Delta=\bestSvdDelta$,
$p=\bestSvdP$); below that, reduction is significantly \emph{worse}. What it
buys instead is diversity and catalogue coverage. Separately, clustering the
latent space recovers genre only partially (ARI $=\ari$, NMI $=\nmi$):
production-defined categories such as hip-hop and EDM are cleanly separable
from audio features alone, while lineage-defined categories such as pop, rock
and R\&B are not. The taxonomy is not reproduced. It is reorganised.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recommendation systems for music lean heavily on two signals: genre metadata
and collaborative filtering over listening histories. Both work, and both
inherit assumptions. Genre is a marketing and lineage taxonomy at least as much
as an acoustic one, and collaborative filtering recommends what similar
listeners chose, which entrenches whatever they were offered.

A third signal is available: the sound itself. Streaming platforms expose
per-track acoustic descriptors --- danceability, energy, acousticness, tempo
and others --- computed from the audio. If musical similarity is partly an
acoustic fact rather than purely a social one, those descriptors should support
recommendation on their own, and the structure they induce should tell us
something about how well the genre taxonomy tracks how music actually sounds.

This paper builds such a system and, more importantly, measures it.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A recommendation pipeline built on a from-scratch SVD, with an
        algorithm choice justified by measured numerical accuracy rather than
        convention (Section~\ref{sec:svd}).
  \item Explicit criteria for choosing the latent dimension, and evidence that
        the standard criteria disagree sharply on this kind of data
        (Section~\ref{sec:rank}).
  \item An evaluation harness with three baselines and paired significance
        testing, yielding a negative result about the central method
        (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
  \item A quantitative answer to whether audio features reproduce genre, using
        chance-corrected agreement metrics (Section~\ref{sec:genre}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A note on data.} \label{par:data}
Reproducing this work on the obvious corpus is harder than it looks. The
widely-cited \emph{Spotify Top Songs and Audio Features} collection
\cite{orlandi} is not a published file: the repository contains a notebook that
\emph{builds} a dataset, requiring weekly chart exports from a separate
scraper project as well as personal Spotify API credentials. Spotify has since
restricted the \code{audio-features} endpoint for newly created API
applications, so that corpus is not straightforwardly reconstructible.

All quantitative results below therefore come from a \catalogSource{}
catalogue of $\catalogSize$ tracks with known generative structure, described
in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. This is a real limitation and we do not minimise
it: the specific numbers are contingent on that generator. The \emph{method} is
not, and every figure and number in this paper regenerates from a single
command against any catalogue with the required columns
(Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\subsection{Deduplication is not optional}
\label{sec:dedup}

Chart datasets contain one row per track \emph{per chart week}. A track that
spent eleven weeks in a Top 200 appears eleven times, with eleven nearly
identical feature vectors. This is a trap with real consequences for a
recommender.

The first is redundancy: near-duplicate rows crowd the nearest-neighbour
ranking, so a result list of ten can easily contain only three or four distinct
songs. The second is subtler. A recommender must exclude the seed playlist from
its own results, and the natural implementation excludes by row index. But a
seed occupies many rows, so excluding the one that matched leaves the others
eligible --- and the system returns the user's own input back to them at
similarity $1.000$.

We collapse to one row per $(\text{title}, \text{artist})$, aggregating
features by median so that a single mis-joined row cannot move a track. On our
catalogue this merged $\duplicatesRemoved$ rows.

Deduplicating by platform URI, which is the obvious key and the one upstream
tooling tends to use, is \emph{insufficient}: the same song commonly holds
several URIs across singles, albums, remasters, explicit and clean edits, and
regional releases. Title-and-artist is coarser but closer to the notion of
``song'' that a listener has in mind.

Collapsing also \emph{creates} information rather than discarding it. The
number of merged rows is a chart-persistence count, which we retain as a
popularity signal and use for novelty weighting in
Section~\ref{sec:strategies}.

\subsection{Scaling}

Features are z-scored,
\begin{equation}
  z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma},
\end{equation}
so that each contributes comparably to distance computations. This is not a
formality. Tempo is measured in the hundreds of beats per minute while
danceability lies in $[0,1]$; on raw values, \dominantFeature{} alone accounts
for $\dominantShare\%$ of total variance (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}). Without
scaling, the decomposition would be a study of tempo and essentially nothing
else.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_scaling.png}
  \caption{Feature variance before and after z-scoring. The left panel is on a
  logarithmic axis --- on a linear one the other eight features are invisible.}
  \label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

For heavily skewed descriptors such as speechiness and instrumentalness, which
are near zero for most tracks with a long tail, a robust scaler (median and
interquartile range) is also available; we use z-scoring throughout for
comparability.

\section{Singular value decomposition}
\label{sec:svd}

\subsection{Mathematical background}

For any $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, the singular value decomposition is
\begin{equation}
  A = U \Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}},
\end{equation}
where $U$ is $m \times m$ orthogonal, $\Sigma$ is $m \times n$ diagonal with
singular values in descending order, and $V$ is $n \times n$ orthogonal.

The classical derivation starts from $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$, which is symmetric and
positive semi-definite. The spectral theorem guarantees it an orthonormal
eigenbasis:
\begin{equation}
  A^{\mathsf{T}}A\, v_i = \sigma_i^2 v_i,
\end{equation}
and these $v_i$ form the columns of $V$. For the left singular vectors,
multiplying through by $A$ gives $A v_i = \sigma_i u_i$, so
$u_i = A v_i / \sigma_i$ whenever $\sigma_i \neq 0$. If
$\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$, only the first $r$ singular values are positive
and
\begin{equation}
  A = U \Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}} = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i u_i v_i^{\mathsf{T}},
  \qquad \sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0 .
\end{equation}
The columns of $V$ and $U$ corresponding to non-zero singular values give
orthonormal bases for the row space and column space of $A$ respectively. Those
orthonormal bases are what make the decomposition suitable for projection.

\subsection{Choosing the algorithm}
\label{sec:jacobi}

The derivation above is correct mathematics and a poor numerical recipe.
Forming $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$ squares the condition number: if
$\kappa(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n$, then $\kappa(A^{\mathsf{T}}A) = \kappa(A)^2$.
Singular values below $\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\sigma_1$ are lost to rounding
before an eigensolver ever sees them.

This is not a hypothetical concern for acoustic features, which are strongly
correlated --- energy with loudness, both against acousticness.
Figure~\ref{fig:backend} measures the effect on a matrix with four dominant
directions and five at noise scale: the $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$ route reaches a
relative error of $\eighMaxRelErr$ on the trailing components, while one-sided
Jacobi holds $\jacobiMaxRelErr$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{fig_backend_accuracy.png}
  \caption{Relative error per singular value on a near-collinear matrix. The
  two algorithms are indistinguishable on the leading components and differ by
  eight orders of magnitude on the trailing ones.}
  \label{fig:backend}
\end{figure}

One-sided Jacobi avoids the problem by never forming $A^{\mathsf{T}}A$. It
orthogonalises the columns of $A$ directly through plane rotations
$A \leftarrow A\, J(p,q,\theta)$, each chosen so that columns $p$ and $q$
become mutually orthogonal. Rotations are orthogonal transformations, so the
singular values are preserved exactly at every step. At convergence
$A = U\Sigma$, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_i = \lVert a_i \rVert, \qquad u_i = a_i / \sigma_i,
\end{equation}
with $V$ the accumulated product of rotations. The rotation angle for each pair
comes from the $2\times2$ Gram block, exactly as in the Jacobi eigenvalue
algorithm, but applied one-sidedly. This is the method behind LAPACK's
\code{dgesvj}, and it computes singular values to high \emph{relative} accuracy
\cite{demmel1992}.

\paragraph{A caution about how to measure this.} Reconstruction error does not
reveal the difference. Both algorithms reconstruct $A$ to $\sim 10^{-16}$
relative Frobenius error, because that norm is dominated by the largest
singular values --- an algorithm can mangle every small component and still
appear excellent. It is relative error \emph{per singular value} that
discriminates. Reaching for the obvious metric would hide the problem
entirely.

\subsection{Projection}

We retain the top $k$ right singular vectors and project:
\begin{equation}
  B_k = B \cdot V_k .
\end{equation}
It may seem odd to call a matrix multiplication a projection, but for an
orthonormal basis the two coincide. Projecting a vector $\mathbf{v}$ onto
$\{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_k\}$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{proj}(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}_i)\,\mathbf{w}_i ,
\end{equation}
and collecting the $\mathbf{w}_i$ as columns of a matrix $W$, the coordinates
in that basis are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}} W = \big[(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}_1), \ldots, (\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{w}_k)\big].
\end{equation}
Applied to every row of $B$ at once, this is exactly $B_k = B V_k$.

\paragraph{An identity worth stating.} When $k = n$, $V_k$ is a full orthogonal
matrix, and orthogonal transformations preserve both inner products and norms.
Cosine similarity is therefore \emph{invariant} under the untruncated
projection: an SVD-based recommender at full rank is mathematically identical
to one using no decomposition at all.

Two things follow. First, this is a strong end-to-end correctness check, and we
use it as one in Section~\ref{sec:eval} --- any error in scaling, decomposition
or ranking would break the equality. Second, it sharpens the research question.
Whatever the decomposition contributes, it contributes entirely through
\emph{truncation}. The interesting quantity is not whether to use an SVD but
how much to throw away.

\section{Choosing the number of dimensions}
\label{sec:rank}

The parameter $k$ controls how much information the recommender is permitted to
see, so it deserves an argument rather than a convention. We implement three
standard criteria:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Variance threshold}: the smallest $k$ whose cumulative
        explained variance reaches 90\%. Interpretable, but the threshold is
        arbitrary.
  \item \textbf{Elbow}: the knee of the scree curve, located by maximum
        perpendicular distance from the chord joining its endpoints. No
        threshold to choose, but it presumes a knee exists.
  \item \textbf{Gavish--Donoho}: the asymptotically optimal hard threshold for
        recovering a low-rank signal from i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise, which
        requires no free parameter at all --- the matrix aspect ratio
        determines everything \cite{gavish2014}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_spectrum.png}
  \caption{Scree plot and cumulative explained variance, with each criterion's
  choice marked. They disagree sharply: $k=\kVariance$, $k=\kElbow$ and
  $k=\kGavishDonoho$ respectively.}
  \label{fig:spectrum}
\end{figure}

That disagreement is itself a finding, and it is not a defect in any of the
three rules --- they are answering different questions. Gavish--Donoho asks how
many components exceed what noise alone would produce, and answers
$\kGavishDonoho$. The variance criterion asks how many are needed to
reconstruct the data faithfully, and answers $\kVariance$. When a spectrum has
one dominant component followed by a near-level tail, as ours does
(Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}, left), those questions have very different answers,
and a flat tail is exactly the regime in which retained components are hard to
distinguish from noise. This anticipates the result in
Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\paragraph{Reporting explained variance.} One arithmetic detail is worth
stating because it is easy to get wrong. Explained variance must be denominated
against the \emph{full} spectrum:
\begin{equation}
  \text{EVR}_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \sigma_j^2},
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all $n$ components, not merely the $k$ retained.
Normalising a truncated spectrum against itself makes every configuration
appear to explain 100\% of variance regardless of $k$, which is both false and
conveniently flattering. Our model retains $\retainedVariance\%$ across
$\modelK$ of $\nComponents$ components.

\section{Latent features and explanation}
\label{sec:latent}

Figure~\ref{fig:loadings} shows the loadings --- each latent component
expressed as a weighting of the original descriptors. Because the components
are mutually orthogonal, each captures an uncorrelated axis of acoustic
variation.

Signs are canonicalised: the largest-magnitude entry of each right singular
vector is forced positive. This matters for interpretation. The decomposition
is unique only up to a simultaneous sign flip of $u_i$ and $v_i$, so without
pinning a convention a component described in prose as ``high energy, loud''
can reappear as ``low energy, quiet'' on a different machine or a different
slice of data, making any written interpretation unreliable.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig_loadings.png}
  \caption{Latent feature loadings, the rows of $V_k^{\mathsf{T}}$. Each row is
  one component expressed as a weighting of the original audio features.}
  \label{fig:loadings}
\end{figure}

Orthonormality also gives us explanation for free. For unit-normalised latent
vectors $\hat q$ and $\hat r$, cosine similarity decomposes exactly:
\begin{equation}
  \cos(q, r) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \hat{q}_i \hat{r}_i ,
  \label{eq:explain}
\end{equation}
so the term $\hat q_i \hat r_i$ is precisely the contribution of component $i$
to the match, and the terms sum to the score. Paired with that component's
loadings, this turns every recommendation into a sentence: \emph{matched on the
energy--loudness axis, which both tracks score high on}. A recommender that
reports only a similarity score asks the reader to take the mathematics on
faith; the decomposition needed to avoid that is already computed.

\section{Recommendation strategies}
\label{sec:strategies}

We implement four strategies over the latent representation.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One-per-song} --- one recommendation per seed track, ensuring
        every input contributes and maximising coverage of the playlist.
  \item \textbf{Overall top} --- the globally highest-scoring candidates,
        maximising coherence with whatever dominates the input.
  \item \textbf{Centroid} --- query by the playlist's mean latent vector, its
        ``taste vector''. This differs from averaging per-seed scores: it is
        more sensitive to a playlist that is coherent in direction and less to
        a single outlying seed.
  \item \textbf{Maximal Marginal Relevance} --- greedily select the candidate
        maximising
        \[
          \lambda \cdot \mathrm{rel}(d) \;-\; (1-\lambda)\max_{s \in S}\mathrm{sim}(d,s),
        \]
        so each pick must be both close to the playlist and unlike what has
        already been chosen \cite{carbonell1998}.
\end{itemize}

The first two sit at opposite ends of a relevance--diversity spectrum with
nothing in between: one maximises similarity, the other enforces variety by
construction. MMR makes that trade-off an explicit parameter rather than a
choice of algorithm, and $\lambda$ can be tuned against the metrics in
Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

Per-seed scores are aggregated by \code{max}, \code{mean} or Borda count. The
choice is not cosmetic: under \code{max}, a single atypical seed can dominate
the entire result list, a real hazard for playlists containing outliers or
duplicated entries. Borda count aggregates ranks rather than scores and is
immune to one seed having systematically higher cosine values than another.

We additionally support a per-artist cap, popularity de-biasing (subtracting a
weighted popularity term so that results are not simply the chart re-ordered),
and Rocchio-style negative feedback that steers the query away from tracks the
listener rejects.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Protocol}

There is no ground truth for enjoyment, so we borrow one: tracks sharing an
artist are treated as mutually relevant. For each of $\nQueries$ evaluation
groups we sample three tracks as a query playlist and hold out the artist's
remaining tracks as the relevant set. Seeds are excluded from results, so no
system can score by returning the query.

We report accuracy (hit rate, recall, MRR, NDCG) alongside beyond-accuracy
measures (intra-list diversity, catalogue coverage). Both families are
necessary: a system returning the ten most popular songs for every query can
score respectably on accuracy while being useless, and only coverage and
diversity expose that. Diversity is computed in the shared scaled-feature space
rather than in any model's own latent space, so that no system is graded in the
geometry most flattering to it.

\paragraph{What this protocol is not.} It measures whether a system recovers
known stylistic neighbours from audio alone. It structurally \emph{penalises}
the cross-genre discovery that motivates the project: a genuinely surprising
good recommendation lies outside the ground-truth group and scores as a miss.
The numbers should be read as ``does the latent space encode real structure'',
not as ``is this a good product''.

\subsection{Baselines}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Random} --- the floor. Any metric a system cannot beat here is
        measuring noise.
  \item \textbf{Popularity} --- ignores the query entirely and returns the most
        popular tracks. Deceptively strong on accuracy metrics, which is
        exactly why it belongs.
  \item \textbf{Raw cosine} --- cosine similarity on the nine scaled features,
        with no decomposition. \textbf{This is the control that matters.} The
        entire justification for the SVD is that projecting to a latent
        subspace beats using the features directly, and that is a hypothesis
        rather than a premise.
\end{itemize}

Three findings follow from Table~\ref{tab:eval}.

\paragraph{The pipeline is correct.} \code{svd\_k9} reproduces
\code{raw\_cosine} to every reported digit on every metric
($\Delta = \fullRankGap$). This is the identity predicted in
Section~\ref{sec:svd}: at full rank the projection is an orthogonal rotation,
and cosine similarity is rotation-invariant. It is a demanding end-to-end
check, since an error anywhere in scaling, decomposition or ranking would break
the equality.

\paragraph{Truncation to $k=7$ costs nothing measurable.} $\bestSvd$ scores
$\bestSvdNdcg$ against $\rawNdcg$ for raw cosine. A paired bootstrap over the
shared queries gives $\Delta = \bestSvdDelta$ with a 95\% confidence interval
of $[\bestSvdCiLow, \bestSvdCiHigh]$ and $p = \bestSvdP$. The interval contains
zero: on this evidence the two are indistinguishable. Meanwhile intra-list
diversity rises from $\rawDiversity$ to $\bestSvdDiversity$ and catalogue
coverage from $\rawCoverage$ to $\bestSvdCoverage$. Discarding two dimensions
is free, and it broadens the results.

\paragraph{Aggressive truncation is significantly worse.} For $k \leq 5$ every
comparison against raw cosine is significant at $p < 0.01$, and accuracy falls
monotonically as $k$ decreases.

\paragraph{On pairing.} All systems are evaluated on identical queries, so the
per-query differences share the query-difficulty variance. Testing them as
independent samples would discard that shared structure, overstate the
uncertainty, and risk converting a null result into a spurious one --- or the
reverse. The comparison must be paired, and the bootstrap must resample query
indices rather than each system separately.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The honest summary is that dimensionality reduction is not an accuracy
technique in this setting. It is a diversity technique. There is a regime
($k=7$) where it is free, and below that one trades measurable accuracy for
measurable variety. That may well be the right trade for a discovery product,
where surfacing unfamiliar material is the point --- but it should be made
deliberately rather than assumed, and the shape of the trade is now something
this system can report rather than something its author has to claim.

It is worth stating plainly what this does \emph{not} show. It does not show
the SVD is useless: the full-rank identity means the decomposition is exactly
as good as the raw features by construction, and at $k=\kVariance$ the
representation carries two fewer dimensions than the original $\nComponents$
with no measurable loss, which matters at catalogue scale. Nor does it show
that latent structure is absent --- Section~\ref{sec:genre} demonstrates the
opposite. It shows that on this retrieval task, compression does not buy
accuracy.

\section{Does audio-feature classification reproduce genre?}
\label{sec:genre}

We now return to the question posed in the abstract. Recommendation quality
cannot answer it; clustering can.

We partition the latent space with $k$-means (k-means++ seeding, ten restarts)
into as many clusters as there are genre labels, then measure agreement between
the partition and the labels. The metrics are chosen to be invariant to label
permutation --- cluster 3 has no intrinsic relationship to ``jazz'', so any
measure depending on which integer was assigned to which group is meaningless
here --- and corrected for chance: Adjusted Rand Index \cite{hubert1985} and
Normalised Mutual Information. Purity is reported alongside but not relied
upon, since it can be driven to 1 simply by using more clusters.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{fig_confusion.png}
  \caption{Each latent cluster's composition by genre label. Rows spanning
  several columns are genres the audio features consider interchangeable;
  columns spread across rows are genres the features consider internally
  inconsistent.}
  \label{fig:confusion}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{fig_genre_space.png}
  \caption{The catalogue in its two leading latent dimensions, coloured by a
  genre label the model never received.}
  \label{fig:genrespace}
\end{figure}

Agreement is partial: ARI $=\ari$, NMI $=\nmi$, purity $=\clusterPurity$ and
silhouette $=\silhouette$ across $\nGenres$ genres. The partition
\clusterVerdict{}. Neither extreme holds --- the taxonomy is neither recovered
nor unrelated --- and the structure visible in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} is
considerably more informative than the scalar summaries:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Cleanly separable.} Hip-hop (88\% of its cluster), live
        recordings (87\%) and EDM (78\%) each occupy a cluster of their own.
        These are categories defined by \emph{production}: speechiness,
        audience noise, synthetic timbre. Audio descriptors measure production
        directly.
  \item \textbf{Merged.} Ambient and classical are split across two clusters,
        each of which is a mixture of both. The features cannot separate them
        at all --- both are quiet, acoustic, instrumental and low-energy ---
        even though the two are historically and culturally distinct.
  \item \textbf{Dissolved.} Pop, rock and R\&B form no clusters of their own,
        spreading instead across three mixed groups. These are categories
        defined by lineage, instrumentation convention and marketing, none of
        which the nine descriptors observe.
\end{itemize}

The answer, then, is: \emph{reorganised, but not arbitrarily}. Audio features
impose an axis of production character rather than genre ancestry. Where a
genre label happens to encode production, it survives the translation; where it
encodes history, it dissolves. This is a coherent alternative taxonomy rather
than noise, and it suggests that the disagreements between acoustic similarity
and genre metadata are systematic and predictable rather than incidental.

\paragraph{Caveat.} This result is measured on a synthetic catalogue whose
genre profiles were specified by hand, and the observed merges partly reflect
overlaps in those specifications --- ambient and classical were given
deliberately similar acoustic profiles. The methodology transfers directly;
this particular finding should be re-measured on a real corpus before being
relied upon, and doing so requires one command (Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Implementation notes}
\label{sec:impl}

Every decomposition is implemented from the underlying mathematics: Householder
QR, two symmetric eigensolvers (shifted QR with deflation, and cyclic Jacobi),
one-sided Jacobi SVD, randomized SVD \cite{halko2011}, cosine similarity,
Levenshtein distance for track lookup, $k$-means, and all ranking and agreement
metrics. NumPy supplies array storage and elementwise operations;
\code{numpy.linalg} appears only in the test suite, as ground truth.

Three implementation details proved to matter more than expected, and are worth
recording for anyone building something similar.

\paragraph{Convergence tolerances must be relative.} An iterative eigensolver
that tests convergence by comparing an absolute off-diagonal sum against a
fixed constant will behave differently at different catalogue sizes, because
that sum scales with $\lVert A \rVert$. On a large matrix the tolerance becomes
unreachable and the solver silently exhausts its iteration budget every time.
Both of our eigensolvers scale their tolerance by the Frobenius norm.

\paragraph{Sign conventions must be pinned.} As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:latent}, an unconstrained SVD produces components whose sign
is an artefact of the arithmetic, which makes any prose interpretation of a
loading unreliable across runs.

\paragraph{Terminal output must survive the local encoding.} Catalogue metadata
is full of accented and non-Latin track titles, and a console using a legacy
code page will raise an encoding error rather than print them. All output
degrades to ASCII when the active encoding cannot represent a character, and
continuous integration runs the test suite under a legacy code page as well as
UTF-8.

The system is verified by 326 tests covering reconstruction accuracy,
orthogonality, Eckart--Young optimality of the rank-$k$ truncation,
cross-backend agreement, and the behaviour of each metric at its known
extremes.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

Every figure, table and quoted number in this paper is produced by a single
command, so the document cannot drift from the code that produced it:

\begin{quote}
\code{python paper/build.py}\\
\code{python paper/build.py --data data/songs.csv} \quad\% any catalogue
\end{quote}

The build regenerates each figure, writes the results table, and emits every
quoted quantity as a LaTeX macro that the prose references in place of a
literal. Changing $k$, the scaling, or the ranking strategy and rebuilding
updates the text along with the figures.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Synthetic corpus.} All results come from a generated catalogue
        (see \S\ref{par:data}). Its generative structure is known and
        influences the outcome, particularly the genre merges of
        Section~\ref{sec:genre}.
  \item \textbf{Proxy ground truth.} Shared authorship stands in for relevance.
        It penalises exactly the cross-genre discovery this system aims at, and
        conflates an artist's stylistic consistency with acoustic similarity.
  \item \textbf{Nine descriptors.} The features describe production, not
        melody, harmony, lyrics or song structure. The dissolution of pop, rock
        and R\&B in Section~\ref{sec:genre} may say more about that limitation
        than about the genres themselves.
  \item \textbf{Linearity.} SVD finds the best \emph{linear} subspace. If
        acoustic similarity is governed by a curved manifold, a linear
        projection will miss it, and a nonlinear method might reach a different
        conclusion about the value of reduction.
  \item \textbf{No listener study.} Every claim here is offline. Whether any of
        it corresponds to what a person would enjoy is untested.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to recommend music from acoustic descriptors rather than genre tags,
and to find out whether the resulting organisation of music resembles the
taxonomy we already use.

On the first question the answer is more qualified than we expected.
Dimensionality reduction did not improve retrieval accuracy over the raw scaled
features. At $k=7$ it was free; below that it cost accuracy in exchange for
diversity and coverage. The full-rank identity between the SVD recommender and
plain cosine similarity makes the reason precise: the decomposition contributes
nothing except through truncation, and truncation is a trade rather than a
gain. Reporting this required baselines and paired significance tests, and it
is the kind of result a project without them would not have found.

On the second question the answer is more interesting. Audio features neither
reproduce the genre taxonomy nor scramble it. They reorganise music by
production character --- separating hip-hop, EDM and live recordings cleanly,
merging ambient with classical, and dissolving pop, rock and R\&B entirely.
That reorganisation is a coherent alternative view of a catalogue rather than a
failure to recover the familiar one, and it is the more promising direction for
future work: not better retrieval of what genre already tells us, but
description of the structure genre leaves out.
